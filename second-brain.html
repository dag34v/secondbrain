The 34V Second Brain

Partner working session - June 2026
Connect what we already have. Earn the right to a product.
01 / WHY NOW

AI in VC is mainstream. Studio-native tooling is not.

The window is short, and we are already partway in.

Claim

Build now. AI in VC is mainstream, but no one has built a second brain for an operating studio. 34V has already codified eight playbooks as Claude skills. We are not starting from zero.

Evidence

  • 85% of dealmakers use AI to automate daily tasks, up from 76% a year ago - Affinity 2026 survey.[1]
  • AI now touches sourcing, diligence, monitoring, and LP reporting.[2]
  • Published "AI for VC" tool lists target funds, not operating studios.[3]
  • 34V already runs Claude skills for memos, GTM, market sizing, portfolio updates, IDA office hours, investor updates, competitive mapping, financial models.[4]

Why it beats waiting

Wait one year and the category will be defined by fund tools we have to retrofit. The studio playbook layer is the part no one else can write. If we do not own it, we will rent it.

What 34V should do

Treat the eight skills as v0. Spend the next 90 days connecting them into one system with shared memory, not building new ones.

Speaker notes. The "no studio-native product" point is an observation from scanning catalogs, not a counted statistic. Flag this. Sources [1]-[4].
02 / POLSIA

Polsia is the wrong template for us.

It runs companies for non-operators. Our companies have operators.

Claim

Polsia is impressive but it solves a different problem. It replaces the operator. 34V backs operators. We need leverage on studio judgment, not an AI CEO.

Evidence on Polsia

  • Founder: Ben Broca. Demoed on Latent Space (Swyx), Feb 26 2026.[1]
  • $1M ARR on day of interview, 30 days after launch; later interviews cite $6M+ ARR and 5,000-6,000 companies.[1][4]
  • Loop: AI CEO wakes nightly, executes, emails the founder each morning.[2]
  • Pricing: subscription plus a 20% cut of revenue.[3]
  • Polsia provisions everything (email, Render, Neon, Stripe, GitHub). The user does not run a company; they steer a bot.[3]

Why this is not our shape

Operators reject "AI CEO". The valuable surface for 34V is not execution-by-bot, it is the studio's compounded judgment delivered to a founder who is already executing. Our bottleneck is not headcount inside the company - it is replaying our playbook across the portfolio.

What 34V should do

Steal one idea from Polsia - the daily nudge loop - and reject the rest. Position as a co-pilot, not a CEO. Live inside the founder's existing tools (Slack, email, doc).

Speaker notes. The brief's "$1.8M / 2,000+" anchor falls between two verified extremes. Deck cites the verified ones. Sources [1]-[4].
03 / THE FLYWHEEL

The flywheel is the moat. A fund cannot copy it. Neither can a SaaS vendor.

Partner judgment goes in. Founder decisions come out. Both feed memory.

1. InPartner judgment, transcripts, updates, KPI deltas.
2. SynthesiseBrain returns the studio's answer with provenance.
3. DecideFounder decides and executes. Outcomes captured.
4. CorrectPartners correct the brain. Memory updates.

Evidence the studio model already compounds

  • Studio companies reach Series A at 72% vs 42% for traditional startups, in 25.2 months vs 56 - GSSN data.[1]
  • Studios codify playbooks, networks, and institutional knowledge as the durable intangible asset.[2]
  • Studios take 30-60% equity and embed teams, vs ~10-20% for a lead VC - structurally closer to the operator.[3]

Why a fund or a SaaS vendor cannot copy this

  • A fund sees decks and KPIs, not daily decisions.
  • A SaaS vendor has no partner-side judgment. They can serve the form; they cannot generate the substance.
  • The corrections only exist if there are operators making decisions and partners watching them. The data is a by-product of the studio model, not the product.
Speaker notes. GSSN figures appear in third-party citations, not a single primary GSSN URL we could verify - flag this caveat. Sources [1]-[4].
04 / THE DATA SUBSTRATE

The brain runs on data we already produce - and currently throw away.

No new founder homework. Wire what exists.

What feeds the brain

  • Fireflies transcripts - partner-founder calls, IC discussions, investor meetings. Decisions and commitments live here.
  • Founder email threads - operator-level signal that no monitoring tool sees.
  • Monthly portfolio updates - written narratives, KPI dashboards, headcount changes.
  • Office-hours minutes (IDA format) - already a skill output. Already structured.
  • Investor updates we send to LPs - already a skill output. Already structured.
  • Financial models - per portfolio company, with actuals vs plan.
  • The eight skills' own outputs - memos, GTM, market sizing, competitive maps, 90-day plans.[4]

What this is not

Not a new dashboard for founders to fill in. Not a new tool for partners to log into. Almost every input above is already being produced this week, for other purposes. The substrate exists; we are not asking for it. We are wiring it.

The hard part is not collection

It is consent and provenance. Each input has a different sensitivity, a different owner, a different question about what crosses between companies. Most failed knowledge systems get the plumbing right and the consent wrong.

Why it beats the alternative

Tools like Standard Metrics and Affinity own the structured surface (KPIs, contacts). They do not see the unstructured substrate where the actual operator reality lives - the transcripts, the emails, the office-hours notes. That asymmetry is the only place where a small studio can out-leverage a billion-dollar SaaS incumbent.[5][6]

What 34V should do

  • Cataloguing pass first. Two weeks, no code: map every existing input, owner, format, refresh cadence.
  • Consent terms with each portfolio founder on what flows in and what can be re-surfaced to other companies. Versioned, opt-in by default.
  • Connect Fireflies, Slack/email, and the skills' outputs first. Skip KPIs in v1 - Standard Metrics will plug in later.
Speaker notes. The point that lands hardest: this is plumbing, not product, and the consent layer is the real design work. If we get that wrong, none of the use cases on the next three slides matter.
05 / FOR THE STUDIO TEAM

For the studio side: a partner who has read everything and forgets nothing.

Six use cases. Ordered by frequency, not ambition.

Office-hours prep. Two minutes before a founder call, the brain summarises: what we said last week, what they committed to, what changed in their KPIs since, which of their open issues match patterns we've seen in other companies. Partner walks in with the room loaded.
Pattern surfacing. Co A and Co B both hit a hiring bottleneck at month 6. Co C is at month 5. The brain flags it and pulls what worked at A and B - before the partner asks.
Conversation continuity. Fireflies transcripts mean the brain remembers commitments across all four partners. No more "wait, did we tell them yes on the bridge?" - the brain knows and links to the timestamp.
Investor-update synthesis. KPI deltas + partner commentary + new risks across the portfolio, into a first-draft LP letter in ten minutes. Partner edits, sends.
Knowledge-debt detection. Brain notices that the pricing playbook hasn't been touched in nine months while four companies asked pricing questions this quarter. Pings the playbook owner.
Diligence acceleration on new builds. Market sizing, competitive map, and GTM scaffold for a new idea, drawn from prior memos in the same vertical. Two days of work compressed into an afternoon.

Why it beats the alternative

Partner attention is the studio's scarce resource. Every time a partner re-derives context another partner already had, or re-answers a question the studio has already answered, leverage leaks. None of these use cases require autonomy. All of them require memory.

Pilot order

Start with office-hours prep. It is the highest-frequency, lowest-risk use case and the one partners will use daily inside week one. Pattern surfacing is the wow demo; build it second. Investor-update synthesis is the LP-facing win; build it third.

06 / FOR THE FOUNDER

For the founder: the 34V partner who is always on call, with the studio's full memory behind every answer.

Not a substitute for the partner. The partner's reach extended.

"How did Co B handle this?" Founder asks about a specific operator problem - churn spike, payroll in Morocco, switching ad platforms, dealing with the same enterprise buyer. Brain returns the actual answer from a sibling company, with consent gates and the partner who handled it cc'd.
Investor-update drafting. Founder triggers it. Brain pulls metrics, drafts the narrative in the founder's voice, flags what investors usually ask. Founder edits and sends in 15 minutes instead of two hours.
Sales-call review. Founder forwards a Fireflies link. Brain returns "the buyer hesitated at minute 18 on pricing; the objection pattern matches three deals Co C closed by switching to annual prepay; here is the email Co C sent."
Reading the room. "I have my quarterly with Partner X on Friday." Brain returns: three points they pushed on last time, their stated view on this question from prior transcripts, and the version of your update most likely to land.
Hiring shortlist. "I need a fractional CFO with North Africa SaaS experience." Brain returns names the studio has surfaced or worked with before, with one-click warm-intro drafts.
Plan-vs-reality. The brain remembers the founder's own 90-day plan. Each week it asks: "you said X by week 6; you are at week 7 and X is not done. Re-plan or escalate?"

Why it beats "use ChatGPT"

A founder with ChatGPT has access to the world's general knowledge. They do not have access to the other founders in our portfolio. The studio's sibling-company memory is the one thing we can offer them that no AI vendor can. Everything else is table stakes; this is the differentiated surface.

The trust contract

If we get the consent wrong, the founder closes the tab and never opens it again. Build the answer to "what of mine is visible to whom?" before building the answer to "what can the bot do for me?" Default opt-in for the founder, explicit opt-in for anything that crosses companies.

Speaker notes. The vignettes are the slide. If a partner can't picture their own portfolio company opening that exact dialog on a Tuesday, the use case is wrong. Pressure-test each one in the working session.
07 / THE PATTERN ENGINE

The non-obvious value is between the use cases.

Co C's question is answered by Co A's and Co B's experience. That is the engine.

How it actually compounds

  • Every interaction writes back with provenance. Founder question, partner correction, office-hours minute, email reply - all carry timestamp, author, and source doc.
  • Indexing is by problem shape, not keyword. "Hiring a senior salesperson in a market with thin SaaS talent" and "filling a head-of-revenue role in Casablanca" are the same pattern. The brain knows that.
  • Partner corrections back-propagate. When a partner says "for North Africa the answer is X, not the default", the next founder asking gets X.
  • Surfacing is push, not pull. The brain notifies the partner when a pattern matches a new company - before that company asks.

Why this beats the alternatives

  • A monitoring tool sees the numbers, not the conversation. The pattern lives in the conversation. Standard Metrics will tell you Co C's CAC is rising; only the brain knows that Co A and Co B saw the same shape and fixed it by changing channel mix.[5][6]
  • A general LLM has no memory of your portfolio. Network-position research in VC finds 67% of predictive power comes from network-derived features.[20] The pattern engine is how we make our network legible to the brain.
  • A junior associate has no leverage. A pattern engine writes the same memo against the full portfolio in seconds. Junior time goes to corrections, not retrieval.

The unavoidable hard problem

Pattern surfacing is the highest-value feature and the highest-risk one. The moment Co C sees an answer that came from Co A's experience, the question is: did Co A consent? Did the partner approve the cross? Was anything identifying leaked? Build the consent UX before the alerting UX. Otherwise the first cross-company surface is also the last.

What 34V should do

  • Define three consent tiers in week one: (1) verbatim with attribution, (2) anonymised pattern, (3) studio-internal only.
  • First demo target: one cross-company pattern alert end-to-end. If we can wire one, we can wire ten.
  • Reserve the right to refuse to surface some patterns. Not everything that compounds value should be surfaced. Partner judgment is the gate.
Speaker notes. If the deck has one slide that justifies the 90-day build, this is it. The pattern engine is what makes this a studio brain rather than a fancy chatbot. Sources [5]-[6] on monitoring landscape; [20] on network-position predictive power.
08 / THE CO-PILOT DIAL

Start advisory. Earn the right to operational.

Autonomy is a dial, not a switch.

Advisory (start here)

Brain proposes. Human chooses. Brain never sends, books, or moves money. Every action has a human in the loop.

Examples: office-hours prep, draft updates, pattern alerts, hiring shortlists, "how did Co B handle this?"

Assisted (after 30 days)

Brain executes reversible, low-stakes actions on a single human approval. One click between brain and the world.

Examples: schedule a meeting, send a templated intro, log a Fireflies action item to a doc, file an LP-update first draft.

Operational (earned)

Brain acts on a category of tasks without per-action review. Promoted only when each gate clears.

Examples: rebudget ad spend across channels, auto-reply to FAQ founder questions, run an outreach sequence to a defined list.

The four gates for promotion

  • High-volume - the task repeats enough that automation pays back.
  • Reversible - mistakes are undoable in minutes, not weeks.
  • Auditable - every action carries a who/why/source trail.
  • Trust-tested - two founders ran it in advisory for 30 days, zero overrides.

Evidence we are not making this up

  • Only 21% of enterprises have mature governance for autonomous agents.[14]
  • The AURA framework maps liability directly to delegated authority and reversibility.[15]
  • Legal exposure compounds across user, configurator, and vendor as autonomy increases.[16]
Speaker notes. Sources [14]-[16]. The four gates are the artefact the partners should be able to recite by the end of the session.

10 / STRUCTURE

Build internal first. Instrument the data. Keep spinout optionality.

You earn the right to a product. You do not design it up front.

A. Internal IP recommend

Tool built and used inside 34V. Not sold.

Fastest. Tight feedback loop. Compounds directly into portfolio returns. No GTM tax.

B. Spinout

Productise and sell to other studios / VCs.

Real revenue. External validation. But portfolio management software is a $3.94B market growing 11.3% CAGR[23] with funded incumbents. Slack, Basecamp, Asana all began internal then spun out[21][22] - the external product was always the second act, not the first.

C. Studio-of-studios

License the playbook + tooling to other regional studios.

Capital-light. Leverages the Morocco-to-global brand. But requires a product already proven against our own portfolio. C is the bet B unlocks; it is not the starting point.

Recommendation

Option A now, with the data plumbing required to switch to B or C later. 90 days. Connect the eight skills. Build the data substrate from slide 4. Ship use cases 1-3 from slide 5 and 1-2 from slide 6. One pattern alert end-to-end.

What "keep optionality" means concretely

  • Multi-tenant data model from day one (one tenant = 34V).
  • Per-skill provenance and feedback capture, exportable.
  • IP assignment terms in employment contracts that allow a future entity to receive the work product.
Speaker notes. The three concrete commitments at the bottom right are the load-bearing part. Without them, "internal first" becomes "internal forever". Sources [21]-[23].
11 / FAILURE MODES

The risks are human. They are not technical.

Shelfware kills more internal tools than bad code does.

The three failure modes

  • Founders do not adopt. They keep using their group chat with the partner instead.
  • Playbooks are too idiosyncratic to reuse. Every company turns out to be a special case and the pattern engine never lands.
  • No one owns maintenance. Playbooks rot. The brain gives confidently wrong answers because nobody updated the source.

Evidence these are the real risks

  • ~70% of digital transformations miss objectives; Gartner finds only 48% fully meet targets.[24]
  • Inadequate change management drives 42%+ of enterprise software failures.[25]
  • 63% of organisations cite underutilised apps as a top consolidation driver; 25-30% of IT spend wasted.[26]

Why these beat the obvious risks

"Will it hallucinate?" is a 2024 question. The 2026 question is whether a founder opens it on a Tuesday morning, and whether the playbook is still true six months later. Hallucination is mitigated by retrieval and review. Adoption and maintenance are mitigated by ownership and rituals - which we do not yet have.

What 34V should do

  • Embed in existing rituals - office hours, weekly founder check-ins, IC prep. No new tool to open.
  • Name a playbook owner per skill, accountable for quarterly review.
  • Define adoption KPIs before launch: weekly active partners, weekly active founders, correction-rate trend.
