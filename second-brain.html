The 34V Second Brain

Partner decision session - June 2026
Connect what we already have. Earn the right to a product.
01 / WHY NOW

AI in VC is mainstream. Studio-native tooling is not.

The window is short, and we are already partway in.

Claim

Build now. AI in VC is mainstream, but no one has built a second brain for an operating studio. 34V has already codified eight playbooks as Claude skills. We are not starting from zero.

Evidence

  • 85% of dealmakers use AI to automate daily tasks, up from 76% a year ago - Affinity 2026 survey of ~300 firms.[1]
  • AI now touches sourcing, diligence, monitoring, and LP reporting.[2]
  • The published "AI for VC" tool lists target funds, not operating studios.[3]
  • 34V already runs Claude skills for memos, GTM, market sizing, portfolio updates, IDA office hours, investor updates, competitive mapping, and financial models.[4]

Why it beats waiting

Wait one year and the category will be defined by fund tools we have to retrofit. The studio playbook layer is the part no one else can write. If we do not own it, we will rent it.

What 34V should do

Treat the eight skills as v0. Spend the next 90 days connecting them into one system with shared memory, not building new ones.

Speaker notes. The "no studio-native product" point is an observation from scanning published catalogs, not a counted statistic. Flag this as a market-mapping read, not a number. Sources [1]-[4] in sources.md.
02 / POLSIA

Polsia is the wrong template for us.

It runs companies for non-operators. Our companies have operators.

Claim

Polsia is impressive but it is solving a different problem. It replaces the operator. 34V backs operators. We need leverage on studio judgment, not an AI CEO.

Evidence on Polsia

  • Founder: Ben Broca. Demoed on Latent Space (Swyx), Feb 26 2026.[1]
  • $1M ARR on day of interview, ~30 days after launch; later reports cite $6M+ ARR and 5,000-6,000 companies.[1][4]
  • Loop: AI CEO wakes nightly, executes, emails the founder each morning.[2]
  • Pricing: subscription plus a 20% cut of revenue.[3]
  • Polsia provisions everything (email, Render, Neon, Stripe, GitHub) - the user does not run a company; they steer a bot.[3]

Why this is not our shape

Operators reject "AI CEO". The valuable surface for us is not execution-by-bot, it is the studio's compounded judgment delivered to a founder who is already executing. The bottleneck at 34V is not headcount in the company - it is replaying our playbook across the portfolio.

What 34V should do

Steal one idea from Polsia - the daily nudge loop - and reject the rest. Position as a co-pilot, not a CEO. Frame it inside the founder's existing tools (Slack, email, doc).

Speaker notes. The brief's anchor of "$1.8M ARR / 2,000+ companies" sits between two verified data points ($1M / 1,000 at launch; $6M+ / 5,000-6,000 weeks later). We cite the verified extremes rather than the unverifiable middle. Sources [1]-[4].
03 / THE FLYWHEEL

The flywheel is the moat. A fund cannot copy it. Neither can a SaaS vendor.

Partner judgment goes in. Founder decisions come out. Both sides feed memory.

Claim

The defensible asset is the loop: partner judgment shapes the answer, founders decide and execute, usage produces data, partner corrections improve the next answer. None of that is available to someone who is not embedded as a co-builder.

Evidence

  • Studio companies reach Series A at 72% vs 42% for traditional startups, in 25.2 months vs 56 - GSSN data.[1]
  • Studios codify playbooks, networks, and institutional knowledge as the durable intangible asset.[2]
  • Studios take 30-60% equity and embed teams, vs ~10-20% for a lead VC - structurally closer to the operator.[3]
  • Studios run ~5 builds at once with the same team and reuse infrastructure.[4]

Why a fund or a SaaS vendor cannot copy this

  • A pure fund does not see operator-grade data. It sees decks and KPIs, not the daily decisions.
  • A SaaS vendor does not have the partner-side judgment that shapes a "good" answer. They can serve the form; they cannot generate the substance.

What 34V should do

Instrument every skill so the partner correction and the founder outcome both flow back to memory. The capture mechanic is the product. Without it we are building search.

Speaker notes. The GSSN figures appear in third-party citations rather than a single GSSN URL we could verify directly; flag this caveat. Sources [1]-[4].
04 / WHERE TO BUILD

Memory is the moat. Co-pilot is the wedge. Monitoring is commoditised.

Build order matters more than scope.

Claim

Three layers: Memory (the studio brain), Co-pilot (the surface founders touch), Monitoring (dashboards and KPI capture). The moat is the first. The wedge is the second. The third we should buy or integrate, not build.

Evidence that monitoring is solved

  • Standard Metrics serves 100+ VC firms and 10,000+ companies with standardised data flows and benchmarking.[1]
  • Chronograph serves clients managing $4T+ in private capital, with audit, validation and LP reporting built in.[2]
  • Affinity owns relationship intelligence as CRM and is the default for sourcing.[3]

Why this beats "build it all"

Monitoring is a race against three funded incumbents; we do not win that race and it is not where our edge sits. The operator playbook layer is where the studio is exposed and where no one has shipped a credible product.[4]

What 34V should do

Order: (1) wire the eight skills into a shared memory with provenance; (2) ship one founder co-pilot surface (office-hours assistant); (3) integrate Standard Metrics or equivalent for monitoring. Do not build (3).

Speaker notes. The "Memory layer is partly built" point ties back to slide 1: the eight skills already contain the playbook artifacts. We need provenance, retrieval, and feedback, not new playbooks. Sources [1]-[4].
05 / THE CO-PILOT DIAL

Start advisory. Earn the right to operational.

Autonomy is a dial, not a switch. Set it to "suggest" first.

Claim

An AI that advises a founder is low-risk and high-trust. An AI that acts on her behalf - books ads, sends outreach, moves money - is high-risk and brittle. We start advisory across the portfolio. We move task-by-task to operational only where the volume, reversibility, and audit trail justify it.

Evidence

  • Only 21% of enterprises have mature governance for autonomous agents.[1]
  • The AURA framework maps liability directly to delegated authority and reversibility.[2]
  • Legal exposure compounds across user, configurator, and vendor as autonomy increases.[3]
  • Copilots are the documented path that lets teams observe AI capability before granting autonomy.[4]

Why this beats "agentic from day one"

One unrecoverable mistake across the portfolio kills founder trust faster than any feature wins it back. Starting advisory is not timidity; it is the only sequence where the data we collect is good enough to earn autonomy later.

What 34V should do

Default: human-in-the-loop. Promote a task to operational only when (a) it is high-volume, (b) reversible, (c) auditable, and (d) two founders have used the advisory version for 30 days without overrides. Track those four gates explicitly.

Speaker notes. The "dial" framing is the most important governance decision in the deck. Tying the promotion criteria to measurable gates is what stops "advisory" from becoming permanent timidity. Sources [1]-[4].

07 / STRUCTURE OPTIONS

Three ways to own this. They are not equivalent.

Internal IP, spinout, or studio-of-studios.

Option What it is Upside Downside
A. Internal IP Tool built and used inside 34V. Not sold. Fastest. Tight feedback loop. No GTM tax. Compounds directly into portfolio returns. No outside revenue. Stays a cost center until proven.
B. Spinout Productise and sell to other studios / VCs. Slack, Basecamp and Asana all started internal then spun out.[1][2][3] Real revenue. External validation. Strategic asset separable from the fund. Portfolio management software is a crowded $3.94B market growing at ~11.3% CAGR[4] - we would compete with funded incumbents. Sales drag pulls focus off the studio.
C. Studio-of-studios License our playbook + tooling to other regional studios; take fees or carry. Capital-light. Leverages the brand of "Morocco to global". Network effects on data. Requires the product to be proven first. Sales cycle with sophisticated buyers. Reveals our edge.
Speaker notes. Present these fairly. Each could be right at different times. The next slide takes a position; this one just frames the choices. Sources [1]-[4].
08 / RECOMMENDATION

Build internal first. Instrument the data. Keep spinout optionality.

You earn the right to a product. You do not design it up front.

Recommendation

Option A now, with the data plumbing required to switch to B or C later. Spend the next 90 days connecting the eight Claude skills into one system with shared memory, provenance, and a single founder co-pilot surface. Do not start a separate product entity yet.

Why not B (spinout-first)

Spinout-first means designing for hypothetical external buyers before we have ten weeks of internal data. The crowded monitoring market[4] punishes generic products. Internal-first is how Slack, Basecamp and Asana actually got built[1][2][3] - the external product was the second act.

Why not C (studio-of-studios)

A studio cannot license a playbook it has not yet stress-tested against its own portfolio. C requires the product proof B never gets to. We can keep C on the table; we cannot start there.

What "keep optionality" means concretely

  • Multi-tenant data model from day one (one tenant = 34V).
  • Per-skill provenance and feedback capture, exportable.
  • License terms in employment contracts that allow assignment to a future entity.
Speaker notes. "Keep optionality" is the load-bearing commitment. Without the three concrete items in the bottom-right block, "internal first" becomes "internal forever". Sources [1]-[4].
09 / FAILURE MODES

The risks are human. They are not technical.

Shelfware kills more internal tools than bad code does.

Claim

Three failure modes matter more than the tech: (1) founders do not adopt; (2) our playbook is too idiosyncratic to reuse across the portfolio; (3) the playbooks rot because no one owns maintenance.

Evidence

  • ~70% of digital transformations miss their objectives; Gartner finds only 48% fully meet targets.[1]
  • Inadequate change management drives 42%+ of enterprise software failures; 55% over budget, 68% over schedule.[2]
  • 63% of organisations cite underutilised apps as a top consolidation driver; 25-30% of IT spend is wasted.[3]
  • Shelfware is overwhelmingly an adoption / ownership problem, not a feature problem.[4]

Why these risks beat the obvious ones

"Will the model hallucinate?" is a 2024 question. The 2026 question is: will the founder open it on a Tuesday morning, and will the playbook still be true six months from now? Hallucination is mitigated by retrieval and review. Adoption and maintenance are mitigated by ownership and rituals - which we do not yet have.

What 34V should do

  • Embed the co-pilot inside existing rituals: office hours, weekly founder check-ins, IC prep. Do not ask founders to visit a new tool.
  • Name a single playbook owner per skill, accountable for quarterly review. Treat playbooks like code: they need a maintainer or they die.
  • Define adoption KPIs before launch: weekly active partners, weekly active founders, correction-rate trend.
Speaker notes. The cheapest mistake we can make is treating this as an engineering project. The expensive one is treating it as one and learning it was a change-management project six months in. Sources [1]-[4].
10 / DECISION

The decision we are asking for today.

One yes, one owner, one date.

Decision requested

Approve a 90-day internal build that connects the eight existing Claude skills into one system - shared memory, provenance, a single founder co-pilot surface, and the optionality scaffolding from slide 8.

Scope explicitly excludes: building portfolio monitoring, building Polsia-style autonomous agents, or starting a spinout entity.

Concrete next step (this week)

  • Owner: name a single partner accountable for the 90-day build.
  • Pilot: pick one portfolio company already using office-hours rituals.
  • Date: v1 live in 30 days; review at 60; first adoption KPI cut at 90.

What "yes" buys us

  • A working second brain across the portfolio.
  • The data to decide, with evidence, between B and C in Q4.
  • A defensible position before the category gets defined by someone selling to funds.

What "no" costs us

Nine months from now we will be evaluating other people's products built on data we should already own. The skills will still exist, but disconnected. The flywheel will not have started.

Speaker notes. Close on the date, not the vision. The single measurable commitment that matters is v1 in 30 days. Everything else flows from that.
