The 34V Second Brain

Partner working session - June 2026

Connect what we have. Earn the right to a product.

01 / Why now

AI in VC is mainstream. Studio-native tooling is not.

Affinity's 2026 survey of about 300 dealmakers puts AI adoption at 85%, up from 76% a year ago. Sourcing, diligence, monitoring, LP reporting - all touched. But that whole tool stack is built for funds. None of it is built for an operating studio.

That's the gap, and I think the window on it is short. Wait twelve months & the category gets defined by Affinity, Standard Metrics, Chronograph. We end up retrofitting their products to a model they weren't designed for.

The good news is we are not starting from zero. We already run eight Claude skills today: investment memos, GTM and 90-day plans, market research and sizing, portfolio updates, IDA office-hours minutes, investor updates, competitive and investor mapping, financial models. That is the v0 of the second brain. Nobody else can write our playbooks for us. If we do not own that layer, we rent it.

So: 90 days to connect what we have. Not to build new playbooks.

notes The "no studio-native product" claim is an observation from scanning Affinity's tool guide and Standard Metrics' tech-stack lists, not a counted statistic. Worth flagging in the room.

02 / Polsia

Polsia is the wrong template for us.

Ben Broca's Polsia is the most-cited reference in this space right now, so worth being precise about why it is not what we want to build. He went live on Latent Space with Swyx in February. $1M ARR thirty days after launch. Later interviews report $6M+ ARR and 5,000 to 6,000 companies. The loop: an AI CEO wakes nightly, executes against the goal, emails the founder in the morning. Polsia provisions everything - email, Render, Neon, Stripe, GitHub. Pricing is subscription plus 20% of revenue.

It is a sharp product. It is just not for our audience. Polsia is built for people who want to push a button and have a company run on their behalf. Our portfolio has operators. They do not want an AI CEO. They want leverage on the playbook the studio has built.

I think we steal exactly one thing from Polsia, which is the daily nudge loop, and reject the rest. Position the brain as a co-pilot that lives in Slack, email, and the docs founders already use. Not a new entity that demands attention.

notes The brief's "$1.8M ARR / 2,000+ companies" anchor sits between two verifiable extremes ($1M / 1,000 at launch, $6M+ / 5,000-6,000 a few weeks later). We cite the verifiable ones.

03 / The flywheel

The flywheel is the moat. A fund cannot copy it. Neither can a SaaS vendor.

Partner judgment goes in. Founder decisions come out. Both feed memory. That is the whole loop, and the reason it is not trivially copyable is that you only get the data if you are embedded as a co-builder.

The studio model already compounds on this. GSSN data via Bundl: studio companies hit Series A at 72% versus 42% for traditional, and they hit it in 25.2 months versus 56. Mandalore's piece on intangible assets makes the same point from a different angle - the studio's edge is the codified playbook plus the network plus the institutional memory. None of that lives inside a fund.

A fund sees decks and KPIs. They do not see the daily decisions. A SaaS vendor can serve the form of an investment memo, but they cannot write the substance, because they do not have the partner-side judgment that decides what is good. We do. That is the asset we are trying to instrument.

notes GSSN numbers appear in third-party citations (Bundl, M Accelerator) rather than a primary GSSN page we could verify directly. Flag this.

04 / What feeds it

The brain runs on data we already produce. We throw most of it away.

Fireflies sits on every partner call we run - founder check-ins, IC discussions, investor meetings. That is where decisions and commitments actually live. The transcripts already exist; nobody reads them twice.

Founder email threads carry operator-level signal that no monitoring tool sees. Monthly portfolio updates are written narratives plus KPIs. Office-hours notes already come out in IDA format because we built that skill. Investor updates we send to LPs are structured outputs too. Financial models exist per company with actuals versus plan. Then there are the eight skills' own outputs - memos, GTM plans, sizings, maps.

None of this is new founder homework. Nothing here is a "fill out this form" surface. It is wiring.

The hard part is not collection. It is consent. Every input has a different sensitivity and a different owner. Fireflies transcripts are the most valuable input we have, and also the one founders will care most about. Draft consent terms in week one, signed before any cross-company surface goes live. Most failed knowledge systems get the plumbing right and the consent wrong.

Order in v1: Fireflies plus the skills' outputs first. Skip KPI capture in v1 and integrate Standard Metrics if we ever need it.

notes The slide that lands hardest in this deck. The takeaway is that the build is plumbing, and the consent layer is the real design work.

05 / For the studio team

The partner who has read everything and forgets nothing.

If this works, the brain becomes that partner. Six places I think it lands first, ordered by frequency and not by ambition.

  1. Office-hours prep. Two minutes before a founder call, the brain summarises what we said last week, what they committed to, what changed in their KPIs since, and which of their open issues match patterns we have seen in other companies. Partner walks in loaded.
  2. Pattern surfacing. Co A and Co B both hit a hiring bottleneck at month 6. Co C is at month 5. The brain flags it and pulls what worked for A and B, before the partner asks.
  3. Conversation continuity. Fireflies transcripts mean the brain remembers commitments across all four partners. No more "wait, did we tell them yes on the bridge?" - the brain knows and links to the timestamp.
  4. Investor-update synthesis. KPI deltas plus partner commentary plus new risks across the portfolio, into a first-draft LP letter in ten minutes. Edit and send.
  5. Knowledge-debt detection. Pricing playbook has not been touched in nine months while four companies asked pricing questions this quarter. Brain pings the playbook owner.
  6. Diligence acceleration on new builds. Market sizing, competitive map, GTM scaffold for a new idea, drawn from prior memos in the same vertical. Two days of work into an afternoon.

Pilot order is (1), then (2), then (4). One is the highest-frequency daily use. Two is the demo that wins partners over. Four is the LP-facing win that justifies the build out loud.

06 / For the founder

The 34V partner who is on call at 11pm, with the studio's full memory behind every answer.

Not a substitute for the partner. The partner's reach extended. Six things a founder can ask the brain for:

  1. "How did Co B handle this?" Churn spike, payroll in Morocco, switching ad platforms, dealing with the same enterprise buyer. The founder asks; the brain returns the actual answer from a sibling company, with consent gates, and the partner who handled it cc'd.
  2. Investor-update drafting. Founder triggers it. Brain pulls metrics, drafts the narrative in the founder's voice, flags what investors usually ask. Founder edits and sends in fifteen minutes instead of two hours.
  3. Sales-call review. Founder forwards a Fireflies link. Brain returns "the buyer hesitated at minute 18 on pricing; the objection pattern matches three deals Co C closed by switching to annual prepay; here is the email Co C sent".
  4. Reading the room. "I have my quarterly with Partner X on Friday." Brain returns the three points they pushed on last time, their view on this question from prior transcripts, and the version of the update most likely to land.
  5. Hiring shortlist. "I need a fractional CFO with North Africa SaaS experience." Brain returns names the studio has surfaced or worked with before, with warm-intro drafts.
  6. Plan vs reality. The brain remembers the founder's own 90-day plan. Each week: "you said X by week 6; you are at week 7 and X is not done. Re-plan or escalate?"

The thing this beats is "go use ChatGPT". A founder with ChatGPT has the world's general knowledge. They do not have the other founders in our portfolio. That is the one thing we can offer them that no AI vendor can.

The trust contract matters here. Get the answer to "what of mine is visible to whom?" right before the answer to "what can the bot do for me?" Default opt-in on the founder's own data. Explicit opt-in for anything that crosses companies.

07 / The pattern engine

The non-obvious value sits between the use cases.

The use cases on slides 5 and 6 are real, but each one alone is incremental. The compounding sits between them. Co C's question gets answered by Co A's and Co B's experience. That is the engine.

It works because the brain indexes by problem shape, not by keyword. "Hiring a senior salesperson in a market with thin SaaS talent" and "filling a head-of-revenue role in Casablanca" are the same pattern. The brain knows that. Partner corrections back-propagate: when a partner says "no, the North Africa answer is X, not the default", the next founder asking gets X. Surfacing is push, not pull - the brain notifies the partner when a pattern matches a new company before that company asks.

This is where the monitoring incumbents fall short. Standard Metrics will tell you Co C's CAC is rising. Only the brain knows Co A and Co B saw the same shape and fixed it by changing channel mix. The conversation is where the pattern lives, and the monitoring tools do not see the conversation.

There is a piece of network-position research on VC outcomes that is directionally relevant - 67% of the predictive power on investment success comes from network-derived features, 84.7% AUC on the model (ResearchGate). The network is the asset. The pattern engine is how we make our network legible to the brain.

The unavoidable hard problem is that pattern surfacing is also the highest-risk feature. The moment Co C sees an answer that came from Co A, the question is did Co A consent. Build the consent UX before the alerting UX. Three tiers in week one: verbatim with attribution, anonymised pattern, studio-internal only. The first cross-company surface that ships is the one founders judge the whole system on.

And - we should be willing to refuse to surface things. Not every pattern that would compound value should be surfaced. Partner judgment is the gate.

08 / The dial

Start advisory. Earn the right to operational.

Autonomy is a dial, not a switch. Start at advisory: the brain proposes, the human chooses. The brain does not send, book, or move money in v1. Anything that touches the world has a human in the loop.

After thirty days of use, we look at moving specific tasks one notch up. Assisted means the brain executes on a single human approval - schedule a meeting, send a templated intro, log a Fireflies action item to a doc. One click between the brain and the world.

Operational is earned task by task. The brain runs categories of work without per-action review - rebudget ad spend across channels, auto-reply to FAQ founder questions, run an outreach sequence to a defined list. We promote a task only when four things are true: it is high-volume, mistakes are reversible in minutes, every action carries an audit trail, and two founders ran it in advisory for thirty days without overrides.

The reason to be patient on this is concrete. Only 21% of enterprises have mature governance for autonomous agents (Atlan). AURA-style frameworks (arXiv 2510.15739) map liability directly to delegated authority. One unrecoverable mistake across the portfolio kills founder trust faster than any feature wins it back.

09 / The gap

Everyone owns the data layer. Nobody owns the playbook layer.

Short version: the existing stack is built around portfolio data and relationships. None of it captures the conversation, the correction, or the playbook.

The column that matters is the third one. Standard Metrics, Chronograph and Affinity all sit on the structured surface. They cannot see the substrate from slide 4, and they cannot get it without a studio's embedded position. That is the asymmetry we can run at.

10 / How we own this

Build internal first. Instrument the data. Keep spinout optionality.

Three structural options. I think A now, with B and C kept open.

Internal IP. Tool built and used inside 34V. Not sold. Fastest, tightest feedback loop, compounds straight into portfolio returns. The downside is no outside revenue and it stays a cost center until we prove it.

Spinout. Productise and sell to other studios and VCs. Real revenue, external validation. But the portfolio-management software market is $3.94B in 2026 growing 11.3% CAGR (Grand View), with funded incumbents. Slack, Basecamp, and Asana all started internal then spun out - the external product was always the second act. Designing for hypothetical buyers before we have run the thing on our own portfolio is the failure mode.

Studio-of-studios. License the playbook and tooling to other regional studios for fees or carry. Capital-light, plays the Morocco-to-global angle well. But requires a product already proven against our own portfolio, which is the proof that spinout-first never gets to. C is the bet that B unlocks, not the starting point.

So: A for 90 days. Connect the eight skills. Build the substrate from slide 4. Ship studio use cases 1, 2, and 4 from slide 5, and founder use cases 1 and 2 from slide 6. One end-to-end pattern alert from slide 7.

Keeping optionality means three concrete things, none of which cost much if we do them now:

  1. Multi-tenant data model from day one, with 34V as the only tenant.
  2. Per-skill provenance and feedback capture, exportable.
  3. IP assignment terms in employment contracts that allow a future entity to receive the work.

11 / What kills this

The risks are human, not technical.

The honest version is that the failure modes worth taking seriously are not "will the model hallucinate?" Three that matter:

  1. Founders do not adopt. They keep using the group chat with the partner. The brain is open in a tab nobody clicks. Shelfware. The pattern is well-documented - 63% of organisations cite underutilised apps as a top consolidation driver, and 25-30% of IT spend is wasted on tools nobody uses (Integrate.io).
  2. The playbooks are too idiosyncratic to reuse. Every company turns out to be a special case. The pattern engine never lands because there are no patterns. This is the one I would watch hardest, because we will not know until v1 ships and we try to surface the first cross-company alert.
  3. Nobody owns maintenance. The pricing playbook is fine in month 1, stale in month 9, actively wrong in month 18. The brain gives confident wrong answers because nobody updated the source.

"Will it hallucinate?" is the wrong worry. That is a 2024 question and the answer is retrieval-with-citations. The 2026 questions are whether a founder opens it on a Tuesday morning, and whether the playbook is still true six months later. Both are change-management problems, not engineering ones. ~70% of digital transformations miss their objectives (MeltingSpot); 42%+ of enterprise software failures trace to inadequate change management (Sci-Tech Today).

What we do about it: embed the brain inside existing rituals (office hours, founder check-ins, IC prep) so there is no new tool to open. Name one playbook owner per skill, accountable for quarterly review. Define adoption KPIs before launch - weekly active partners, weekly active founders, correction-rate trend. If those numbers move the wrong way at sixty days, we know.

12 / The decision

One yes, one owner, one date.

What we are asking for today: approve a 90-day internal build. Connect the eight skills. Wire the substrate from slide 4. Ship the studio use cases on slide 5 and the founder use cases on slide 6. One pattern alert end-to-end. Advisory mode only in v1, with the gates from slide 8 reserving the right to promote later.

Out of scope: portfolio monitoring (we integrate Standard Metrics if we ever need it), Polsia-style autonomous agents, any spinout entity.

Three concrete steps this week:

  1. One partner named accountable for the 90-day build.
  2. One portfolio company picked as the design partner - someone already in the office-hours rituals.
  3. Consent terms drafted in week one, signed by all portfolio founders before any cross-company surface lights up.

Dates: v1 live in 30 days, review at 60, first adoption KPI cut at 90.

The cost of "no" is concrete too. Nine months from now we evaluate other people's products built on data we should already own. The eight skills still exist, just disconnected. The flywheel from slide 3 never starts. Partner judgment stays trapped in eight people's heads.
