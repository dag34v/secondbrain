02 / Polsia

Polsia is the most-cited reference in this space. It is not our shape.

Reference
Polsia

Ben Broca (solo). Demoed on Latent Space with Swyx, Feb 26 2026.
Traction
$1M ARR at launch. $6M+ ARR & 5-6,000 companies a few weeks later.

Stack

Sharp product. Wrong audience.

What's defensible about Polsia is the friction it removes. Most "AI for business" products break on the first integration. Polsia provisions the whole stack, so the user never sees the seams. The daily email creates a habit loop the user opts into voluntarily. The 20% rev cut aligns incentives.

What's not defensible is the playbook. The same template runs across all 5,000+ companies. There is no partner judgment shaping the answer, no co-build relationship, no operator decisions feeding the next decision. It's a generic founder-in-a-box.

That's why Polsia works for the audience it has - people who want a company to push a button on. It is also why it does not work for ours. 34V backs operators. They don't want an AI CEO. They want the studio's hard-won playbook plus sibling-company memory, delivered to them on demand, with a partner still in the loop.

The one thing worth borrowing: the daily nudge loop. It's the cheapest behaviour-change mechanic in the space.

06 / For the studio team

The partner who has read everything and forgets nothing.

1

Office-hours prep

Two minutes before a founder call: what we said last week, what they committed to, what changed in their KPIs since, which open issues match patterns from other companies. Partner walks in loaded.

2

Pattern surfacing

Co A & Co B both hit a hiring bottleneck at month 6. Co C is at month 5. The brain flags it and pulls what worked for A and B - before the partner asks.

3

Conversation continuity

Fireflies transcripts mean the brain remembers commitments across all four partners. No more "did we tell them yes on the bridge?" The brain knows & links to the timestamp.

4

LP update synthesis

KPI deltas + partner commentary + new risks across the portfolio, into a first-draft LP letter in ten minutes. Edit & send.

5

Knowledge-debt alerts

Pricing playbook hasn't been touched in nine months while four companies asked pricing questions this quarter. Brain pings the playbook owner.

6

Diligence acceleration

Market sizing, competitive map, GTM scaffold for a new idea, drawn from prior memos in the same vertical. Two days of work into an afternoon.

Pilot order --> ship #1 first (highest frequency, partners use it daily). #2 second (the demo that wins partners over). #4 third (the LP-facing win that justifies the build out loud).

The 34V partner who is on call at 11pm, with the studio's full memory behind every answer.

"How did Co B handle this?"

Churn spike, payroll in Morocco, switching ad platforms. Founder asks; brain returns the actual answer from a sibling company - with consent gates and the partner who handled it cc'd.

2

4

Reading the room

"I have my quarterly with Partner X on Friday." Brain returns the three points they pushed on last time, their view on this question from prior transcripts, and the version of the update most likely to land.

5

Hiring shortlist

"I need a fractional CFO with North Africa SaaS experience." Brain returns names the studio has surfaced or worked with before, with warm-intro drafts.

6

Plan vs reality

The brain remembers the founder's own 90-day plan. Each week: "you said X by week 6; you're at week 7 and X isn't done. Re-plan or escalate?"

What this beats: "go use ChatGPT". A founder with ChatGPT has the world's knowledge. They don't have the other founders in our portfolio. That's the one thing we can offer that no AI vendor can.

08 / The trust contract

Get "what of mine is visible to whom?" right before "what can the bot do for me?"

The first time Co C sees an answer that came from Co A, every founder asks the same question: did Co A consent? Did the partner approve the cross? Was anything identifying leaked? Get this wrong on day one and founders close the tab forever.

1

Verbatim with attribution

The actual answer from Co A, with their name on it. Requires explicit per-question opt-in from Co A.

2

Anonymised pattern

"A sibling company in your stage hit this and resolved it by X." No identifying detail. Default for most surfaces.

3

Studio-internal only

Pattern surfaces to partners, never to founders. For anything sensitive enough that founders shouldn't see it.

Three tiers in week one. Founders pick the tier per data type. Partners reserve the right to refuse to surface anything - not every pattern that would compound value should be surfaced. Partner judgment is the gate.

14 / What kills this

The risks are human. They're not technical.

1

Founders don't adopt

They keep using the group chat with the partner. The brain sits in a tab nobody clicks. Shelfware.

63% of orgs cite underutilised apps as a top consolidation driver. 25-30% of IT spend wasted (Integrate.io).
2

Playbooks too idiosyncratic

Every company turns out to be a special case. The pattern engine never lands because there are no patterns to surface.

The one I'd watch hardest. We won't know until v1 ships and we try to surface the first cross-company alert.
3

Nobody owns maintenance

Pricing playbook fine in month 1, stale in month 9, actively wrong in month 18. Brain gives confident wrong answers.

~70% of digital transformations miss objectives (MeltingSpot). 42%+ of failures trace to change management (Sci-Tech Today).

"Will it hallucinate?" is the wrong worry. That's a 2024 question. The 2026 questions are whether a founder opens it on a Tuesday morning, and whether the playbook is still true six months later.

What we do about it --> embed the brain inside existing rituals (office hours, founder check-ins, IC prep). Name one playbook owner per skill. Define adoption KPIs before launch - weekly active partners, weekly active founders, correction-rate trend.

Appendix A / Anticipated questions - strategy & economics

If you were sitting on the other side of the table.

"The 20-25% EBITDA stat is PE. Our portfolio is early-stage. Why cite it?"

Directional, not literal. Our companies don't have EBITDA to optimise - they have founder-hours and decision-velocity to reclaim. The PE benchmarks tell us the size of the lever, not the unit of measurement. We measure adoption in those units, not P&L points.

"Could Affinity or Standard Metrics ship a 'studio mode' and close this gap overnight?"

They can ship a feature. They can't ship a flywheel. The missing piece is the operator data they structurally can't get - Fireflies transcripts, IC discussions, office-hours notes from an embedded co-build relationship. A fund-shaped tool with a studio toggle still reads as a fund tool to a founder.

"What stops another studio building the same thing on the same Claude/MCP stack?"

Nothing technical. But what they get is their portfolio's brain, not ours. High Alpha could ship identical plumbing tomorrow - they end up with High Alpha's playbook, not the North-Africa-operator playbook we've spent years writing. Defensibility is the data, not the code.

"What if Polsia or one of the YC companies pivots toward studios?"

They'd have to redesign, not retarget. Polsia's 20% rev cut, the provisioning model, and the AI-CEO loop are all wrong for our audience. By the time they re-architect, we're 6-12 months in with operator data they don't have. The threat is real but not fast.

"Do we actually have enough portfolio companies for pattern density?"

Patterns surface after 3-4 sibling data points on the same operator problem. We already have those for the common ones - hiring, pricing, channel switching, payroll in Morocco. Density isn't the bottleneck. Capture is. The patterns are there; the layer that surfaces them isn't.

"Does this conflict with our 20% equity + $200k follow-on economics?"

Amplifies them. Every partner hour we reclaim goes back to portfolio support or new builds. Studio math: more co-built companies per partner per year, at higher early-stage success rate. The brain raises the ceiling on what a four-partner team can co-build.

"What's the v1 envelope in real numbers?"

One partner at ~25% time, one engineer for 90 days, tooling in the low five figures monthly. If the team grows past two engineers we are doing the wrong thing. Small teams build memory products; large teams build dashboards. We are not standing up a product org.

Appendix B / Anticipated questions - execution, risk & governance

The questions that decide whether v1 ships.

"Have founders been consulted on consent? Will they actually opt in?"

Not yet. Week one of v1 is consent conversations with every portfolio founder, before any wiring. If three say no, we redesign. If five say no, we ship the single-company version only and reconsider cross-company surfaces in v2.

"What if the brain gives a confident wrong answer in a high-stakes moment?"

Exactly why v1 is advisory only. The brain never sends, books, or commits anything. Every answer carries provenance to the source doc. Founders are told upfront it's a first draft. The blast radius in v1 is one person reading a wrong sentence, not a portfolio company losing money.

"What if our playbooks turn out to be too idiosyncratic to reuse?"

Second-biggest risk on slide 14, and one we only learn from v1. The test: ship the first cross-company pattern alert in 90 days and watch. Two land --> the thesis holds. Zero land --> we kill v2 and re-scope. We don't pre-commit to the answer.

"What's the legal exposure if data crosses companies the wrong way?"

Three layers. (1) Consent terms drafted with counsel in week one. (2) Anonymised pattern is the default tier - no identifying detail. (3) Audit trail on every cross-company surface. The brain doesn't act in the world in v1, so the worst case is rolling back one surface, not unwinding a transaction.

"Why won't this become shelfware like every internal tool?"

Because it lives inside existing rituals - office hours, IC prep, founder check-ins - not as a standalone tab. The first time a partner sees the office-hours prep land in their inbox two minutes before a call, they don't go back. Adoption is mechanical, not motivational.

"Who owns the playbook? Will they have enough authority?"

Named partner per skill, accountable for quarterly review. Authority sits with whoever already owns the underlying workflow. If pricing is X's domain, X owns the pricing playbook in the brain. We are not creating new roles - we are wiring the ones we have.

"Who has kill authority? When does it fire?"

The named build owner. Trigger is the 60-day review against pre-set adoption KPIs - weekly active partners, weekly active founders, correction-rate trend. If those move the wrong way, the owner winds it down. No further IC required.

"If we keep B and C optionality, are we signalling we're not committed to A?"

No. Optionality in v1 = three lines in employment contracts + a multi-tenant data model. Both free now, catastrophically expensive in year two. Keeping options isn't hedging - it's refusing to foreclose them before we have data.
